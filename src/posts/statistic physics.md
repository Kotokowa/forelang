Yes — but there is a small misconception worth correcting first.

**arXiv is not primarily a site for textbooks.** It is a preprint repository, so most papers are research papers. However, many theoretical physicists upload **lecture notes, summer-school lectures, pedagogical reviews, and book-length notes** there. For the topics you mentioned, this is actually one of the best ways to study.

And for your interests — **condensed matter → phase transitions → field theory → topology/critical phenomena** — there is a particularly good set of authors.

I would rank them roughly like this:

---

## 1. David Tong — probably the best starting point

David Tong

If you haven't studied his notes yet, **start here**. His notes are unusually well-written and are designed to teach rather than merely document someone's research.

### Statistical Physics

[David Tong — Statistical Physics](https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is an undergraduate/final-year course, but the phase-transition section is particularly useful:

* Ising model
* mean-field theory
* critical exponents
* 1D Ising model
* Peierls argument
* Kramers–Wannier duality
* Landau theory
* Landau–Ginzburg theory
* fluctuations and correlations

The course explicitly progresses toward **Landau-Ginzburg theory**, making it an excellent bridge from statistical mechanics to field theory. ([大卫·汤][1])

### Quantum Field Theory

[David Tong — Quantum Field Theory](https://davidtong.org/teaching/quantum-field-theory/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is a standard graduate-level introduction:

> classical field theory → canonical quantization → interacting fields → Dirac field → QED

It is not specifically condensed matter QFT, but it gives you the machinery you'll repeatedly encounter in many-body theory. Tong also provides videos of the lectures. ([大卫·汤][2])

### Quantum Hall Effect

This one **is on arXiv**:

**David Tong, "Lectures on the Quantum Hall Effect"**

[arXiv:1606.06687](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06687?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is particularly relevant to your interests because it connects:

**Landau levels → Berry phase → quantum Hall effect → Chern-Simons theory → edge states → topology.**

Tong explicitly assumes QFT knowledge and aims the lectures at graduate students. ([arXiv][3])

### Why I recommend Tong so strongly

His teaching sequence is almost exactly what I would recommend for you:

**Statistical Physics → Statistical Field Theory → QFT → Quantum Hall Effect**

His teaching page also contains a dedicated **Statistical Field Theory** course whose goal is explicitly to understand phase transitions, critical phenomena and the renormalization group. ([大卫·汤][4])

---

# 2. Subir Sachdev — extremely important for quantum phase transitions

Subir Sachdev

If Tong teaches you **what the machinery is**, Sachdev teaches you **what condensed-matter physicists actually do with it**.

He is one of the central figures in quantum criticality and strongly correlated systems.

### A very good entry point

**Quantum phase transitions of antiferromagnets and the cuprate superconductors**

[arXiv:1002.3823](https://arxiv.org/abs/1002.3823?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is a pedagogical review covering:

* quantum phases
* quantum phase transitions
* antiferromagnets
* spin liquids
* Schwinger bosons
* gauge theory
* superconductors
* Dirac fermions
* quantum criticality

It's especially valuable because it demonstrates how **field theory emerges naturally from condensed matter problems**, rather than teaching QFT as an isolated mathematical formalism. ([arXiv][5])

And there is a much newer set of lectures:

**Lectures on the quantum phase transitions of metals**

[arXiv:2407.15919](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.15919?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This gets into Fermi surfaces, fractionalized Fermi liquids, strange metals and quantum criticality. ([arXiv][6])

### Sachdev is especially worth following if you want

**quantum phase transition → quantum criticality → emergent phenomena → strongly correlated matter.**

---

# 3. Flavio Nogueira — phase transitions from the field-theory viewpoint

This one is almost exactly what you asked for.

**Flavio S. Nogueira, "Introduction to the field theory of classical and quantum phase transitions"**

[arXiv:1009.1603](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.1603?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The abstract describes it explicitly as a **self-contained introduction to field-theoretic methods for classical and quantum phase transitions**. ([arXiv][7])

This is the kind of thing I would read **after Tong's Statistical Physics**.

The conceptual progression is approximately

$$
\text{statistical mechanics}
\rightarrow
\text{Landau theory}
\rightarrow
\text{field theory}
\rightarrow
\text{RG}
\rightarrow
\text{critical phenomena}.
$$

For your particular interest in **TPT / critical dynamics**, this is probably more directly useful than a generic condensed-matter textbook.

---

# 4. Eduardo Fradkin — condensed matter field theory

Eduardo Fradkin

This is someone I would definitely put on your reading list.

**Field Theoretic Aspects of Condensed Matter Physics: An Overview**

[arXiv:2301.13234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.13234?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is newer and gives a broad picture of how QFT has become central to condensed matter.

It covers things including:

* phase transitions
* quantum phase transitions
* topological phases
* dualities
* field-theoretic descriptions of condensed matter

([arXiv][8])

Fradkin is also the author of the famous textbook **Field Theories of Condensed Matter Physics**. So this arXiv article is a nice way of seeing his viewpoint before diving into the full book.

# 5. Michael Levin / Xiao-Gang Wen — if you become interested in topological phases

Xiao-Gang Wen

Wen is one of the most important people in modern condensed matter theory, particularly:

**topological order → emergent gauge fields → anyons → quantum information → topological phases.**

His papers are not necessarily beginner-friendly lecture notes, though.

For example:

**Detecting topological order in a ground state wave function**

[arXiv:cond-mat/0510613](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0510613?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is a research paper rather than a textbook, but it illustrates the kind of conceptual leap you eventually want to make: topological phases cannot always be classified by conventional Landau symmetry breaking. ([arXiv][9])

I'd put Wen **later**, rather than starting with him.

---

# 6. A very useful "middle ground": Arouca, Cappelli & Hansson

If you eventually become interested in the relationship between **QFT and topology**, this is quite nice:

**Quantum Field Theory Anomalies in Condensed Matter Physics**

[arXiv:2204.02158](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.02158?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

It pedagogically introduces anomalies and then connects them to:

* quantum Hall systems
* Fermi liquids
* Weyl semimetals
* topological insulators
* topological superconductors

It assumes basic QFT, including fermions, gauge fields, path integrals and functional methods. ([arXiv][10])

This is **not** where I'd begin, but it becomes very interesting once you know QFT.

# What about Kardar?

Mehran Kardar

**Absolutely study him.**

In fact, David Tong himself recommends Kardar's:

* *Statistical Physics of Particles*
* *Statistical Physics of Fields*

on his statistical physics course page. Tong describes the first as a modern approach and the second as covering critical phenomena. ([大卫·汤][1])

Kardar is particularly valuable because his treatment of statistical physics naturally leads toward **field theory and RG**.

The distinction I'd make is:

| Author       | Best for                                               |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Tong**     | Learning the concepts cleanly                          |
| **Kardar**   | Statistical physics + field theory + RG                |
| **Nogueira** | Field theory of phase transitions                      |
| **Sachdev**  | Quantum phase transitions / strongly correlated matter |
| **Fradkin**  | Modern condensed-matter field theory                   |
| **Wen**      | Topological order / emergent phenomena                 |

# If I were designing your study path

Considering that you've already been interested in **Ising models, minimal models, TPT, Berry phase and CFT**, I actually wouldn't recommend simply reading a generic "Condensed Matter I → II" textbook.

I'd do this:

### Stage 1 — Statistical mechanics

**David Tong — Statistical Physics**

[Tong's Statistical Physics notes](https://davidtong.org/teaching/statistical-physics/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Read especially:

$$
\boxed{\text{Ising model → critical exponents → Landau theory → Landau-Ginzburg}}
$$

Tong's phase-transition section is remarkably compact while still covering the essential ideas. ([DAMTP][11])

---

### Stage 2 — Statistical field theory

Then move to:

**Nogueira — Introduction to the field theory of classical and quantum phase transitions**

[Nogueira's lecture notes](https://arxiv.org/abs/1009.1603?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

At this point you should start thinking of

$$
Z=\int\mathcal D\phi\,e^{-S[\phi]}
$$

as the natural language of statistical mechanics rather than something belonging exclusively to particle physics.

---

### Stage 3 — Renormalization group

Then study **Kardar's Statistical Physics of Fields**.

This is where the idea

$$
\text{microscopic details}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{coarse graining}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{fixed point}
\quad\longrightarrow\quad
\text{universality}
$$

really becomes central.

This is arguably the most important conceptual bridge between ordinary statistical mechanics and modern theoretical condensed matter.

---

### Stage 4 — Quantum field theory

**David Tong — QFT**

[Tong's QFT lectures](https://davidtong.org/teaching/quantum-field-theory/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Don't necessarily try to memorize all of QED. For condensed matter, pay particular attention to:

* path integrals
* symmetries
* Noether theorem
* spontaneous symmetry breaking
* effective actions
* renormalization
* fermionic fields
* gauge fields.

### Stage 5 — Quantum condensed matter

Then:

**Sachdev + Fradkin**

Sachdev → quantum criticality

Fradkin → field-theoretic condensed matter

and eventually:

**Tong's Quantum Hall lectures**

[Tong — Quantum Hall Effect](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06687?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Then you're naturally entering:

\boxed{
\text{QFT}

## One important point about "downloads and reviews"

I **wouldn't use download count alone to rank arXiv notes**. arXiv is fundamentally a preprint server, and download numbers are not a reliable measure of pedagogical quality. A famous research paper can have enormous traffic while being terrible as a first textbook.

For lecture notes, I'd weight things approximately:

**author's reputation + citations/research impact + pedagogical reputation + completeness + suitability for your level > raw download count.**

By that standard, the names above are unusually strong.
